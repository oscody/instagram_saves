# Four Claude prompts for overcoming procrastination

A five-slide carousel presenting four copy-ready Claude prompts, each framed as a procrastination "advisor" based on Barbara Oakley's claims about the insular cortex and the first 20 minutes of starting a task. The slides include role, task, steps, rules, context fields and output format for each prompt.

Written by claude-haiku-5-5.

## What it teaches

1. Prompt 1, find what you are avoiding: pinpoint the moment right before you avoid the task when resistance is strongest.
2. Separate the task itself from the feeling it triggers (fear of doing it wrong, boredom, overwhelm, or something else).
3. Check whether the same feeling appears in other tasks you avoid, to see if it is a pattern.
4. Name the real thing being avoided in one clear sentence.
5. Base conclusions only on what you described, ask for more information if it is insufficient, and do not accept 'laziness' as an answer.
6. Prompt 2, get through the first 20 minutes: reduce the task to a first action so small that refusing it feels harder than doing it.
7. Define what the first 20 minutes look like, covering only the entry point rather than the whole task.
8. Identify one signal that resistance has faded and you are in flow.
9. Decide in advance what happens right after the 20 minutes to keep momentum going.
10. Prompt 3, stop deciding when to start: identify what already triggers you to attempt the task, if anything.
11. Attach the task to a fixed, repeatable cue that already happens daily, such as a time, a location, or an existing action.
12. Remove any decision points between the cue and the task so they are directly connected.
13. Define what to do if the cue happens but you still feel resistance. Do not rely on remembering or willpower.
14. Prompt 4, get back on track after a slip: identify what a slip usually looks like for you, such as skipping one day, one week, or losing the habit entirely.
15. Define the smallest re-entry action, smaller than the original starting action, that gets you back in without catching up on everything missed.
16. Identify what typically causes the slip so it can be addressed directly next time.
17. Define one signal that tells you that you are fully back on track, not just that you tried once.
18. Do not frame a slip as a personal failure.

## Caption

Barbara Oakley spent years studying why the brain resists starting things it knows it should do. 

What she found comes down to one part of the brain, the insular cortex, the same region responsible for processing physical pain and discomfort.

The moment you think about starting something you have been avoiding, that part of your brain lights up as if you are in actual discomfort. 

That is why procrastination feels so physical. It is not laziness. It is your brain treating the task like a threat.

Here is the part most people never learn. That discomfort is not permanent.

Oakley's research shows it fades within about 20 minutes once you actually begin. The task was never the hard part. The first 20 minutes were.

Most people wait to feel ready before starting. Oakley proved that backwards. You do not think your way into motivation. 

You act your way into it. The moment you start, the resistance that felt unbearable a minute ago begins to disappear on its own.

Claude can now run Oakley's exact framework on whatever you have been avoiding. Find out what you are actually avoiding underneath the task itself. Get through the first 20 minutes using the smallest possible starting action. 

Build a cue so you never have to decide when to start again. And know exactly what to do the next time you slip, so one bad day never turns into giving up completely.

Here are 4 prompts that run the full system on whatever you keep putting off.
