# Dahomey's 1851 attack on Abeokuta

A history carousel narrating King Gezo of Dahomey's 1851 march on Abeokuta, the Amazon warriors, and the Egba commander Balogun Ogunbona's preparations. The last two slides show two books by the account's founder, Adegoke Taiwo.

Written by claude-haiku-5-5.

## Caption

“Destroy Abeokuta and break the pride of the Egba people forever” — King Gezo of Dahomey ..

Before sunrise on March 3, 1851, the people of Abeokuta slept without knowing that one of the most terrifying armies in West African history was already moving through the forests toward their city.

The morning was calm. Traders prepared their stalls. Farmers sharpened their tools. Children chased one another through narrow paths beside the streams of the Egba town. Nothing in the air suggested that blood would soon flow across the land.

But far beyond Ishaga village, hidden behind thick trees and rising dust, the army of Dahomey was marching.

At the front were the feared Amazons of Dahomey, thousands of highly trained female warriors feared across West Africa for their brutality, discipline, and courage in battle. These were not ordinary women. They were warriors raised for war from a young age, trained to survive pain, carry weapons, and kill without hesitation. Armed with muskets, machetes, spears, and battle charms, they marched beside the soldiers of King Gezo with only one mission in mind: destroy Abeokuta and break the pride of the Egba people forever.

As they moved through the forests, villages began to fall in silence. Smoke slowly rose into the sky. Families abandoned their homes and fled in fear. Those who escaped spoke of an army unlike anything they had ever seen before, women dressed for battle, faces hardened like stone, advancing without fear as drums echoed through the trees.

Inside Abeokuta, many leaders initially doubted that Dahomey would launch such a massive attack. The city had become prosperous. Trade was growing. Missionaries preached openly. Life inside the Egba stronghold felt secure behind its rocky hills and defensive walls.

But one man did not ignore the warning signs.

Balogun Ogunbona of Ikija, one of the respected war commanders of Abeokuta, understood the meaning of unusual silence. Without waiting for celebration or public alarm, he quietly gathered his loyal warriors and moved toward the vulnerable parts of the city’s defense near Aro. He trusted experience more than comfort.

Then the attack came.
