# Four Claude prompts for Ferriss's DEAL audit

A carousel with four Claude prompts meant to apply Tim Ferriss's ideas to a business: a life design audit, an 80/20 elimination audit, an income autopilot builder, and a fear-setting audit. The caption retells Ferriss's shift from 80-hour weeks to a passive income and sets out his rule on eliminating, automating and delegating.

Written by claude-haiku-5-5.

## What it teaches

1. Core rule: never automate something that can be eliminated, and never delegate something that can be automated. Eliminate first.
2. Prompt 1, Life Design Audit: review everything about current work, goals and daily life; identify what success looks like and test whether it is genuinely desired or inherited from outside expectations; find the gap between what you are building toward and what you actually want; identify the single biggest mismatch between daily work and the life you are designing toward; state plainly whether current work builds the right thing or optimizes the wrong outcome.
3. Prompt 2, 80/20 Elimination Audit: review all current tasks, clients and commitments; classify each as high-output (top 20%) or low-output (bottom 80%) based on actual results; identify tasks already automated or delegated that should be eliminated entirely; find the 20% of clients or commitments causing 80% of stress and wasted time; state what gets cut before anything else is touched.
4. Prompt 3, Income Autopilot Builder: take the top 20% of tasks and revenue activities that survived elimination; identify which can be turned into a repeatable process without daily decisions; identify which need delegation to a person or tool and what that handoff looks like; find the single highest-leverage automation that frees the most time while protecting the most income; describe the autopilot state, meaning what the business looks like running without daily involvement.
5. Prompt 4, Fear-Setting Audit: identify the changes the first three steps call for that have not been acted on; for each, name the specific fear underneath the inaction rather than the excuse; define the worst realistic outcome if that fear came true; identify how you would recover if the worst case happened; state plainly whether the cost of inaction is lower than the cost of the feared outcome.
