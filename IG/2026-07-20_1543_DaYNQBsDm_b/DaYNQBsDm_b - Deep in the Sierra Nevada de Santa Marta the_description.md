# The Kogi People and the Mamo Tradition

The post describes the Kogi, Indigenous people of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta in Colombia, and their mamos, who are raised in darkness for nine years and see the world through Aluna. It also recounts their 1990 and 2012 documentary warnings, which urge people to remember the invisible and care for the source.

## Caption

Deep in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, the highest coastal mountain range in the world, rising sheer from the Caribbean coast of northern Colombia, live the Kogi — an Indigenous people who consider themselves the *Elder Brothers* of humanity. They are the descendants of the ancient Tairona civilization, which retreated into the mountains during the Spanish conquest in the 16th century and survived largely untouched by the outside world for more than four hundred years. The Kogi believe they were entrusted by the great mother of creation, Aluna, with the sacred responsibility of keeping the balance of the earth.

At the heart of Kogi society are the *mamos* — the priests, seers, and spiritual leaders of the people. To become a mamo, a young boy is selected in early childhood and taken deep into a cave or dark hut, where he is raised for the first nine years of his life in near-total darkness, in the company of his teachers. He is fed simple foods, taught the sacred cosmology of his people, and trained to perceive the world entirely through Aluna — the invisible realm of thought, intention, and living spirit that the Kogi believe is the true reality behind the material world. Only after nine years is the young mamo brought out into the sunlight and shown the physical world for the first time. He sees it as an already-initiated being, understanding it as a reflection of the deeper realm he has been studying.

The Kogi believe the Younger Brothers — the rest of humanity — have forgotten how to see Aluna, and that the destruction of the natural world is the visible consequence of a spiritual forgetting. In 1990 and 2012, Kogi elders broke their isolation to deliver urgent warnings to the wider world through documentary films. The message was the same. Remember the invisible.
