# Nietzsche's Herd Mentality and Three Claude Prompts

The carousel explains Nietzsche's idea of the herd mentality and self-overcoming, then presents three prompts written to be run in Claude. The prompts cover auditing inherited beliefs, finding an avoided self, and building a daily practice toward it.

Written by claude-haiku-5-5.

## What it teaches

1. Prompt 1, Inherited Belief Audit: list your current goals, standards and definition of success, then ask for each whether you chose it deliberately or absorbed it from family, culture or fear of judgment.
2. Name each inherited belief specifically rather than as vague 'external pressure', and treat your most automatic defense as evidence to examine.
3. Prompt 2, Avoided Self Finder: identify a change you keep postponing, a risk it would require, and the version of yourself that taking that risk would build.
4. State honestly what the avoidance protects (comfort or fear), the concrete cost of continuing to avoid it, and the exact daily moment the avoidance shows up.
5. Prompt 3, Self-Overcoming Protocol: build one specific daily action toward the avoided self and name the discomfort as the mechanism.
6. Write a resistance plan for low-motivation days, define what 30 days of evidence would confirm real movement, and identify the one inherited belief that must shift for the practice to feel natural.
7. If the inherited belief, avoided self or practice is unclear from what you provided, ask for more information before concluding.

## Caption

Nietzsche believed most people never actually become themselves. They live by inherited beliefs, chase goals handed to them by family or society, and defend standards they never actually chose.

He called this the herd mentality — and said escaping it was the hardest and most necessary confrontation a person can have with themselves.

He wrote his entire philosophy while losing his health, his sanity, and nearly everyone who knew him. He believed becoming who you actually are requires exactly that kind of cost — leaving behind what's comfortable, safe, and expected.

Most solopreneurs are running someone else's version of success. Nietzsche would say that's a real failure, not the business not working, but never finding out who you actually are underneath what you were told to want.
